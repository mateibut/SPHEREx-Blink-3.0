        How this search was run

          Source measurements

          Click a dot in the image. We measure its brightness in every frame: spectrum = brightness vs. wavelength (one colour per survey pass), light curve = brightness vs. date (one colour per detector).

          Uncertainty of the spectrum

          Flux = background-subtracted sum in a 2-pixel aperture, in image units (not calibrated). A moving object is only inside the circle on the dates when it was there – that is expected, not an error.

            Distant-body analysis

            Two questions about a distant body. Which motion fits the positions of a candidate: standing still, a straight line, or a straight drift plus the swing of the Earth's orbit? And how likely is this search to find a body of a given distance and brightness in the images on screen? The second one adds synthetic bodies to the real images: it measures the search, it does not find anything.

            Which motion fits?

            Select a candidate in the list (Find movers, or Look for a distant body), then compare.

            How likely is a distant body to be found?

            Needs images of one detector from at least 3 survey passes with real pixels. A body that starts in the middle of the field stays inside it, over a year, beyond about 300 AU in a 30′ field and beyond about 1700 AU in a 5′ field (the column headings of the map give the swing for these images).

              Explain the selected candidate

              An explainer that says only what was measured. Every sentence is followed by the ids of the measured values it is made of, and a check refuses any sentence that holds a number that is not one of them. No language model is used: the sentences are written by rules from the numbers, so it works without a key or a network and sends nothing anywhere.

                  Reproducibility bundle

                  A small JSON file that lets someone else check what was done: the field and detector, the version of every algorithm, the settings of the last search, a SHA-256 hash of the pixels of every image (it proves the same data without publishing a pixel), the images' dates and wavelengths, the candidates, and a link and a command to replay it. It holds no pixel, no file path and no address of this computer; a bundle that fails that check is not saved. It cannot say which IRSA product each cutout came from (the backend does not report it): that field is empty and says why.

                  Start exploring

                  Illustration, not SPHEREx data

                  SPHEREx Blink lets you pick a spot on the sky and flip through infrared images of it taken on different dates. Stars stay put. Things that move or change brightness stand out.

                  1. Pick a targetType coordinates or choose one of the five test objects.
                  2. Blink and compareFlip through the dates, or switch to Difference, Stack and Trail.
                  3. MeasureClick a source to see its spectrum and light curve.
                  4. Check what is knownOpen the sky map to see catalogued objects around it.

                  Why look for movers?

                  Nearby brown dwarfs, fast stars, asteroids and comets reveal themselves by changing position between observations. The same blinking method found Pluto in 1930, and it is how astronomers search for undiscovered worlds such as the hypothetical “Planet X”.

                  About the data

                  The default workspace uses synthetic data that imitates observations. The backend can serve local FITS files. Check the data-source badge for the current field. The real SPHEREx images are public at NASA/IPAC IRSA, and the real-data results page shows earlier measurements (numbers and charts only, no images).

                  Built for a challenge

                  Made for the NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026, “Planet X and SPHEREx”, with AI assistance (Claude Code). The pictures in the header and background are generated artwork, not telescope images.

                  The telescope

                  SPHEREx maps the whole sky in 102 colours

                  SPHEREx is a NASA space telescope that surveys the entire sky in near-infrared light. Instead of one picture, it records 102 different colours of every patch of sky, from 0.75 to 5 micrometres, and it maps the sky again about every six months.

                  • 102colours (wavelength bands)
                  • 0.75–5 µmnear-infrared range
                  • 6detectors, working in pairs
                  • ~6″per pixel
                  • 4all-sky surveys in about 2 years

                  How it sees colour

                  Each detector sits behind a filter whose colour changes gradually across it. A source lands on a different spot in each exposure, so it is measured at a different wavelength every time, and its spectrum is assembled from many exposures. That is why this tool shows the wavelength of every frame.

                  Why it helps find movers

                  Because the sky is mapped again and again, the same spot is imaged on different dates, which is exactly what blinking needs. Cold objects such as brown dwarfs tend to be brightest at the long-wavelength end, so detectors D5 and D6 are the best place to look for them.

                  What else it studies

                  Beyond moving objects, SPHEREx studies how the universe began (cosmic inflation), how galaxies formed and grew, and where water and other ices are found in our galaxy.

                  The six detectors

                  DetectorWavelengthWhat it is good for
                  D10.75–1.09 µmStars and galaxies; cold brown dwarfs are very faint here
                  D21.10–1.62 µmStars and galaxies
                  D31.63–2.41 µmCooler stars
                  D42.42–3.82 µmWater-ice feature near 3 µm
                  D53.83–4.41 µmCold brown dwarfs; CO2 ice near 4.3 µm
                  D64.42–5.00 µmCold brown dwarfs; CO ice near 4.7 µm

                  Detectors work in pairs (D1 with D4, D2 with D5, D3 with D6). The D2 range comes from the IRSA archive documentation; the split between D1 and D3 is approximate.
